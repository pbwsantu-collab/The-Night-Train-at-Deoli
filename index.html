Our Casuarina Tree

By Toru Dutt (1856–1877) — Complete Text, Analysis & Interactive Exam Portal

About the Poet

Toru Dutt (1856-1877) was a pioneering Indian poet and translator, often acclaimed as one of the founding figures of Indo-Anglian literature. Born on March 4, 1856, in Rambagan, Kolkata, into an affluent, highly educated, and culturally progressive Christian family, she was the youngest daughter of Govin Chunder Dutt. Despite living a tragic life cut short at the young age of 21 by tuberculosis, Toru achieved extraordinary literary milestones in both English and French.


Having traveled extensively across France and England during her youth, she possessed a fluent command of French, English, and Sanskrit. Her literary legacy includes "A Sheaf Gleaned in French Fields" (1876), a novel in French titled "Le Journal de Mlle d'Arvers", and her masterpiece volume "Ancient Ballads and Legends of Hindustan" (1882), published posthumously by her father. "Our Casuarina Tree" stands out in this final collection as her most famous autobiographical lyric poem, seamlessly blending Western Romantic aesthetics with deep Indian sensibilities.

About the Poem

"Our Casuarina Tree" is an exquisite 55-line lyric poem structured into five 11-line stanzas. Composed around 1876, it serves as a passionate, nostalgic elegy dedicated to a majestic casuarina tree growing in the garden of Toru Dutt's ancestral home (Baugmaree, Kolkata). More than a physical feature of nature, the tree acts as a living monument connected to her happiest childhood memories and her beloved deceased siblings, Abju and Aru, who both succumbed to illness at early ages.


The poem unfolds through a deliberate progression of thought across its five stanzas:

  • Stanza 1 (Objective Description): Focuses on the grand physical architecture of the tree, its choked embrace by a python-like creeper, its red blossoms, and the wildlife (birds and bees) singing late into the night.
  • Stanza 2 (Dawn Scene & Morning Landscape): Paints a vivid landscape of morning in Bengal — a grey baboon perched on the tree's crest, its offspring playing below, kokilas singing, cows moving to pastures, and white water-lilies blooming on the pond like snow.
  • Stanza 3 (Shift to Subjectivity & Nostalgia): Declares that the tree is dear not merely for its external beauty, but because it is bound up with memories of her departed siblings. The poet hears a mournful, dirge-like murmur from the tree.
  • Stanza 4 (Transcendence Across Space): Describes how this ghostly lament follows her across oceans to distant lands (France and Italy), evoking the inner vision of the tree as she knew it in her native home.
  • Stanza 5 (Consecration & Immortality): The poet seeks to consecrate a verse to honor the tree, invoking the "deathless trees" of Borrowdale in Wordsworth's poetry, hoping that Love will preserve her beloved tree from the curse of Oblivion.

Complete Poem (Click highlighted words for English-Bengali Meaning)

Stanza 1

Like a huge Python, winding round and round
The rugged trunk, indented deep with scars,
Up to its very summit near the stars,
A creeper climbs, in whose embraces bound
No other tree could live. But gallantly
The giant wears the scarf, and flowers are hung
In crimson clusters all the boughs among,
Whereon all day are gathered bird and bee;
And oft at nights the garden overflows
With one sweet song that seems to have no close,
Sung darkling from our tree, while men repose.
Stanza 2

When first my casement is wide open thrown
At dawn, my eyes delighted on it rest;
Sometimes, and most in winter, - on its crest
A grey baboon sits statue-like alone
Watching the sunrise; while on lower boughs
His puny offspring leap about and play;
And far and near kokilas hail the day;
And to their pastures wend our sleepy cows;
And in the shadow, on the broad tank cast
By that hoar tree, so beautiful and vast,
The water-lilies spring, like snow enmassed.
Stanza 3

But not because of its magnificence
Dear is the Casuarina to my soul:
Beneath it we have played; though years may roll,
O sweet companions, loved with love intense,
For your sakes shall the tree be ever dear!
Blent with your images, it shall arise
In memory, till the hot tears blind mine eyes!
What is that dirge-like murmur that I hear
Like the sea breaking on a shingle-beach?
It is the tree's lament, an eerie speech,
That haply to the unknown land may reach.
Stanza 4

Unknown, yet well-known to the eye of faith!
Ah, I have heard that wail far, far away
In distant lands, by many a sheltered bay,
When slumbered in his cave the water-wraith
And the waves gently kissed the classic shore
Of France or Italy, beneath the moon,
When earth lay trancèd in a dreamless swoon:
And every time the music rose - before
Mine inner vision rose a form sublime,
Thy form, O Tree! as in my happy prime
I saw thee, in my own loved native clime.
Therefore I fain would consecrate a lay
Stanza 5

Unto thy honor, Tree, beloved of those
Who now in blessed sleep for aye repose, -
Dearer than life to me, alas! were they!
Mayst thou be numbered when my days are done
With deathless trees - like those in Borrowdale,
Under whose awful branches linger pale
"Fear, trembling Hope, and Death, the skeleton,
And Time the shadow;" and though weak the verse
That would thy beauty fain, oh fain rehearse,
May Love defend thee from Oblivion's curse.

Critical Analysis & Literary Structure

Form & Stanza Pattern

The poem consists of 5 stanzas, each containing 11 lines (Hendecastich). The rhyme scheme follows a unique custom structure designed by Toru Dutt: abbba cdddc e (or abbba cddcee variant), blending the Italian Petrarchan octave/sestet logic with Romantic stanzaic freedom and lyrical fluidity.

Central Thematic Pillars

  • Nostalgia & Loss: Deep emotional yearning for her childhood home and her deceased siblings, Abju and Aru.
  • Immortality through Art: The poet's desire to consecrate a verse to shield the tree from time's decay and forgotten oblivion.
  • Nature as a Living Medium: The casuarina tree functions as an empathetic entity that absorbs human grief and mournfully echoes across oceans.
  • Synthesis of East and West: Harmoniously blends Indian rural imagery (cows, kokilas, water-lilies) with European literary references (Wordsworth's Borrowdale yew-trees).

Detailed Figures of Speech & Rhetoric

Simile "Like a huge Python, winding round and round" Compares the thick, coiling creeper clinging tightly to the rugged bark to a giant python snake.
Simile "The water-lilies spring, like snow enmassed" Compares white water-lilies clustered densely on the pond surface to accumulated white snow drifts.
Simile "Like the sea breaking on a shingle-beach" Compares the whispering, rustling murmur of the tree's leaves to waves crashing on a pebbled coast.
Personification "The giant wears the scarf..." Attributes chivalrous human qualities to the tree, framing it as a heroic giant wearing the creeper like a scarf.
Personification "It is the tree's lament, an eerie speech" Attributes human grief, mourning, voice, and articulate speech to the wind moving through the tree's branches.
Personification "Fear, trembling Hope, and Death, the skeleton, / And Time the shadow" Abstract human emotional and existential concepts are given concrete, personified forms guarding the ancient trees.
Apostrophe "Thy form, O Tree!" / "O sweet companions" Direct, emotionally charged address to an inanimate object (the tree) and absent/deceased loved ones.
Literary Allusion "With deathless trees - like those in Borrowdale" Direct reference to William Wordsworth's Romantic poem "Yew-Trees", aligning her Indian tree with English canonical classics.
Oxymoron / Paradox "Unknown, yet well-known to the eye of faith!" Combines contradictory terms to express how spiritual memory and love transcend physical or geographical separation.
Visual & Auditory Imagery "crimson clusters", "grey baboon", "sweet song that seems to have no close" Engages multiple senses (sight and sound) to create a vivid, immersive atmosphere of Bengal's flora and fauna.
